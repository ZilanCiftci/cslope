cSlope has two kinds of external load, both acting on the ground surface: distributed loads, vertical or normal to the ground, and vertical line loads.

## Distributed loads (UDL)

Open **Model › Loads & support › Distributed loads**. Each row is one load:

| Column | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **q** | Pressure in kPa between x1 and x2 |
| **x1**, **x2** | The horizontal extent of the load, in meters |
| **Direction** | **Vertical**: q per horizontal meter. **Normal to ground**: q acts normal to the ground surface, as water or earth pressure against a slope |

A pressure q normal to ground inclined at β carries q per horizontal meter vertically, as a vertical load would, and q·tan β horizontally, pushing into the ground. On level ground the two directions are the same. Water standing against a slope is the typical case: entered as a normal load q = γw·(water level − ground), it acts as [free water](/docs/water) does on the ground, without pore pressure in the soil. The canvas labels such a load "⊥ ground".

**+ Add UDL** adds a load of 10 kPa from x = 0 to 5 m. On the canvas, select a load and drag its ends horizontally, or drag the load itself to move it whole.

A distributed load imported from a .stix file can carry a **degree of consolidation** per material: the part of the load not yet consolidated, $(1 - U)\,q$, adds to the pore pressure at the slice bases under it in that material, where they are below the water. The UDL window lists these under the table, for example "UDL 1, not consolidated: Material "Clay" 50 % of q (excess pore pressure below the water)"; **Remove** drops one, and the load is then fully consolidated in that material. The PDF report gives them as the degree of consolidation, "degree of consolidation U = 50 % in Clay (the rest of the load as excess pore pressure)".

## Line loads

Open **Model › Loads & support › Line loads**. Each row is a line load **P** in kN per meter run at position **x**. **+ Add line load** adds a load of 50 kN/m at x = 10 m. A selected line load can also be dragged on the canvas.

**Angle from vertical** inclines the load: 0 is vertical, positive turns it towards +x, ±90 is horizontal (a strut or an anchor force on a wall). Its vertical part acts like a vertical load; its horizontal part acts on the slice under the load, at the ground or at **Load point y** when given (a point on a wall face).

**Load point y (m)** is the elevation of the load point, for a load on a wall face; empty (*ground*) puts the load on the ground at x. It is used for an inclined load only: the field is grayed out while the angle is 0, since a vertical load acts the same at any height.

## How loads enter the analysis

A load on the ground above a slice adds to the slice's weight. The horizontal part of a load normal to the ground acts on the slice as a horizontal force at the ground, with its moment about the center of the slip surface. A load outside the sliding mass has no effect on that slip surface. The finite element strength reduction takes a normal load normal to the ground; the limit analysis bounds take its vertical part only. All load values accept [parameters](/docs/geometry#parameters).

With [partial factors](/docs/analysis#partial-factors) switched on, both kinds of load are multiplied by the load factor γQ.

<Callout type="tip">
  A load on the crest usually lowers the factor of safety. A load on the toe, in the resisting part of the sliding mass, can raise it. With undrained soil a load on the resisting side never lowers it.
</Callout>
